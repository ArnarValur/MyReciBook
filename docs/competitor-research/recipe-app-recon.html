The field is not what the badges say

Seven apps, millions of downloads, glossy screenshots, 4.7-star badges. Fourteen research agents went underneath the listings. What they found is a category that is large, wealthy, badly run, and structurally unable to give people what they keep asking for.

Finding 01

The star ratings are lying, and we can prove it

The stores display a recency-weighted average dominated by silent tappers — people who hit five stars in a prompt without typing a word. Pull the written reviews instead and the picture inverts.

ReciMe prompts for a rating during onboarding: 9.5% of their five-star reviews were written before the person had used the app — "just downloaded", "so far so good". And SideChef's headline 4.0 isn't a rating at all. Play localises ratings per storefront, and Norway's histogram resolves to roughly five people. Its US rating is 4.5.

We are not competing against 4.7-star products. We are competing against 4.7-star badges worn by 3.4-star products.

Read the food diary column again. One app has a daily food log, and it is a calorie counter, not a recipe app. Read the pantry column. One app has a pantry, and it hasn't shipped a release since March 2025.

Nobody in this category has combined a personal cookbook with a pantry and a diary. That isn't a gap we found by squinting — it is an entire column of empty boxes.

The reason is worth understanding, because it's structural rather than accidental. Media brands can't let you own recipes, because the catalogue is their only asset. UGC platforms can't, because metering your cookbook is the revenue. Calorie counters don't want recipes, they want logged grams. Nobody is positioned to do all of it — except an app whose customer is the cook.

Finding 04

Data ownership is the category's open wound

The loudest churn moment in every single dossier is a version of "I lost my recipes." COOKmate users on a device change. Tasty users to an unfixed Facebook-login break running since April. SideChef users when Budget Bytes shut down and the notice said their recipes "will not be transferred." Mob users after changing phone.

Tasty has no export, no print, no copy — and deleting your account irreversibly wipes your saves. Cookpad's licence lets them sublicense your recipes for free, waives your moral rights, and survives your account's termination, while the Terms tell you that backing up your content is your responsibility. There is no export feature.

Exactly one competitor can say "your recipes are files you can take with you" — COOKmate, with a daily local backup and a published schema. It is also the app that has survived sixteen years at 4.6 stars. That is not a coincidence.

The unguarded door

Migration in. Users with 500–1500 recipes are trapped by file formats, not loyalty — several COOKmate reviewers report retyping recipes by hand. Nobody in this category has built a good importer for a rival's export. Ingesting COOKmate, Paprika and Recipe Keeper libraries with images and formatting intact takes the category's most valuable users at the exact moment they are angriest.

Finding 05

The incumbents are wobbling — with receipts

Starved

Tasty

BuzzFeed sold ~51% to Byron Allen in May 2026. July: 35% of staff cut, Tasty included. Android sits on v2.16 — two releases this year against eight last year — with listing copy unchanged since 2018.

Frozen

SideChef

No release on either platform since 14 March 2025 — while tightening its paywall server-side and still wearing a Play Editors' Choice badge. Engineering follows the B2B payers, and the payers aren't the users.

Shrinking

Cookpad

Down to 78 employees globally. Revenue falling five straight quarters, operating profit −98% year on year. Premium is unbuyable across the Nordics, Germany and the Netherlands.

Leveraged

Mob

Commercially the healthiest — 280,000 subscribers, $12.5M ARR, #1 top-grossing UK. But negative equity of −£1.30m, cash down to £0.59m, headcount 39→31, funded by revenue-based debt rather than equity.

Acquired

Foodvisor

Bought in January 2026 by Xynergy — owner of the diet brand Comme J'aime — for a reported €50–60M. French press is already raising editorial-independence questions.

The real rival

ReciMe

Genuinely growing and genuinely dangerous. But its Android build is v4.1.2 against iOS v6.1.5, its listing tells Android users the app syncs to "iOS and iPad", and both lifetime giveaways were iOS-only. Android is second-class at our closest competitor — and Android is the category we're entering.

Finding 06

Norway and the Nordics are unserved. Completely.

Cookpad ships 25 languages, none of them Nordic, and won't sell Premium in any Nordic country. COOKmate ships Danish and Finnish but no Norwegian. Tasty is English-only in every storefront on earth, and its Nordic ratings are its lowest worldwide. SideChef speaks English and German, and quotes USD prices in the Norwegian listing while advertising Walmart checkout with no US-only caveat. Mob charges 499 kr a year for UK aisles and UK ingredients.

Every one of these companies treats Norway as a billing currency rather than a market. We live here. Norwegian and Icelandic localisation, metric-native units and Nordic ingredient names are cheap for us and uncontested by everyone.

Finding 07

Rules we're taking from other people's mistakes

  1. Never ship a trial. Trial mechanics are the largest single complaint category across Mob, ReciMe, Foodvisor and SideChef. A one-time price makes them unnecessary.
  2. Make restore-purchase flawless. Under one-time pricing this is existential, not a nicety.
  3. Never take back what people already have. COOKmate killed its $5.99 one-time in 2022 and still carries 790 one-star reviews for it — more than its 2★ and 3★ buckets combined.
  4. Publish the price. Three of seven refuse to. "$25, once, forever" belongs in the first line of the listing.
  5. Never lose a user's data. In any migration, ever. The number one churn trigger in every dossier without exception.
  6. Metric is a unit system, not a toggle. SideChef converts to "237mL of rice" and it costs more stars than not converting at all.
  7. Honour a stated preference everywhere. SideChef's most-repeated complaint, unfixed from 2018 to 2026, is that its allergy filters are ignored. Mob's break on unnormalised ingredients — a reviewer called it "dangerous". This is a safety issue and a free win.
  8. Never score a food as "bad". Foodvisor's red-and-frowny-face scoring draws accusations of feeding diet culture. An app that never moralises about food has a clean position, free.
  9. Don't meter import, and keep the source link. Cookpad monetised imports above five a week and drew a public revolt from food creators.
  10. Never market a feature that only works in one country.
The one real threat to our model

COOKmate did not fail at one-time pricing. It failed because one-time pricing had to fund a permanently recurring cost — 200+ hand-maintained site scrapers plus cloud sync. The developer said so publicly in 2018. Our $25 survives only if our recurring costs stay bounded: parse schema.org generically rather than maintaining a parser per site, keep storage device-local, take on no sync obligation we can't fund forever. This is the constraint that decides whether the business works — and it deserves its own session.

What this changes about the plan

Nothing about the destination. A great deal about the confidence level.

The incumbents are large, tired, and locked into business models that forbid them from doing the three things their own users keep asking for: own your recipes, pay once, and track what you actually eat. We are not late to a crowded market. We are early to an obvious one that nobody with a subscription P&L is allowed to enter.

Bounded recurring cost

Can import work without a maintained-parser treadmill? This decides whether $25-once is a business or a slow bleed.

Migration in

Which rival export formats do we ingest at launch, and how do we reach those users while they're angry?

Nordic localisation

Norwegian and Icelandic at launch, or v1.1?
